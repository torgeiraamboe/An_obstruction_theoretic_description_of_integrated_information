

\section{IIT systems}

In \citeme, Tull and Kleiner develop a category theoretic foundation for integrated information theory, based on symmetric monoidal categories with some extra structure. We will use this as a backdrop for our cohomology theory, hence we quickly review their setup. 

\subsection{Process theories}

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{process theory} is a symmetric monoidal dagger category $(\C, \otimes, \1, \dagger)$, usually denoted simply by $\C$, together with two maps $q_S\: S\to \1$ and $w_S\: \1\to S$ for any $S\in \C$ satisfying monoidality and standard naturality conditions. Objects in $\C$ are interpreted as system types, and morphisms as physical processes. The dagger $\dagger\: \C\op\to \C$ is interpreted as a time-reversal operation. 
\end{definition}

Morphisms of the form $\1 \to S$ will be reffered to as \emph{states}, and we will write $\St(S)$ for the collection of states of $S$. Similarly, morphisms $S\to \1$ will be referred to as \emph{effects} and morphisms $\1\to \1$ \emph{scalars}. The designated maps $q$ and $w$ are called the \emph{discarding effect} and the \emph{noise state} respectively, and are interpreted as such. 

\begin{definition}
    A morphism $f\:S\to S'$ is \emph{causal} if it preserves the discarding effect, 
$$q_{S'}\circ f = q_S,$$
and similarly \emph{cocausal} if it preserves the noise state. 
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    Tull-Kleiner does not assume $\C$ to be a dagger category for their most general setup, but for concretely tying the categorical version to the IIT algorithm of \citeme, as we do in \cref{ssec:obstruction-and-IIT}, this is required. 
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
    The standard example of a process theory is the category of classical probabilistic processes. This is the category $\Class$ of finite sets, where a morphism $S\to S'$ assigns to an element $s\in S$ an 'unnormalized probability distribution' of elements in $S'$, which are maps $S\times S' \to \R^+$. The time reversal operation is given by $f^\dagger(s,s') = f(s',s)$. Composition of two maps $f\: S\times S'\to \R^+$ and $g\: S'\times S'' \to \R^+$, is defined by
    \begin{align*}
        f\circ g \: S\times S'' &\to \R^+ \\
        (s,s'') &\longmapsto \sum_{s'\in S'}f(s,s')g(s',s'').
    \end{align*}
    The monoidal structure is the cartesian product, meaning the unit $\1$ is the singleton set. Product of morphisms is given by 
    $$f\otimes g (s,s'')(s',s''') = f(s,s')g(s'',s''').$$
    The discarding process $q_S$ is the unique process with $q_S(s) = 1$ for all $s\in S$, meaning a process is causal whenever it is stochastic. The noise state is the uniform probability distribution with $w_S(s) = \frac{1}{\vert S \vert}$. 
\end{example}

\subsection{Decompositions and subsystems}

The following definition will be the backbone of our functorial approach to integration in \cref{sec:integrated-cohomology}

\begin{definition}
    Let $\C$ be a process theory, and $S$ an object in $\C$. A \emph{decomposition} of $S$ is a pair of objects $A, B \in \C$ together with a causal isomorphism $\psi\: S \simeq A\otimes B$. We denote decompositions by $(A, B, \psi)$, or sometimes simply by $(A, B)$.
\end{definition}

Any object $S\in \C$ has a decomposition $(S,\1)$, which we call the \emph{trivial} decomposition. We will further say that two decompositions $(A, B, \psi)$ and $(A', B',\psi')$ are \emph{equivalent} if there are causal isomorphisms $f\: A\simeq A'$ and $g\: B\simeq B'$, such that 
$$\psi' = (f\otimes g)\circ \psi.$$
The \emph{complement} decomposition of $(A,B)$ is the same decomposition, just in the opposite order; it is denoted $(A,B)^\perp = (B, A)$. Note that, if two decompositions are equivalent, then so are their complements. 

\begin{definition}
    Let $\D(S)$ denote the set of equivalence classes of decompositions of $S$. The operation $(-)^\perp$ acts on $\D(S)$, and we define a \emph{decomposition set} of $S$ to be a subset $\D \subseteq \D(S)$ that contains the trivial decomposition and is closed under $(-)^\perp$. 
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    Given a decomposition set $\D$ of $S$, and a decomposition $(A, B, \psi)\in \D$, we define the \emph{restricted} decomposition set $\D_{\mid A}$ to be the set of decompositions of $A$ that can be extended to a decomposition of $S$ in $\D$. Furthermore, we define the \emph{restriction} of a state $s\: \1\to S$ to $A$ to be the state defined by 
    $$s_{\mid A} = (\Id_A\otimes q_{B})\circ \psi \circ s \: \1\to A,$$
    and similarly for $B$. 
\end{definition}

Intuitively, the restriction is defined by identifying $S$ with its decomposition, and then discarding the factor we don't want to consider. By functoriality and naturality, such restrictions depend only on the equivalence class in $\D(S)$, and not on the particular representative. 

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{system} in $\C$ is a triple $(S, \D, T)$, where $S\in \C$, $\D$ is a decomposition set of $S$, and $T\:S\to S$ is a causal process, interpreted as a \emph{time evolution}. It is assumed that also $T^\dagger$ is causal. 
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    The most basic example is the \emph{trivial} system $(\1, \{\1\otimes \1\}, \Id_\1)$. 
\end{example}

A state of a system $(S, \D, T)$ is a state of $S$. Given such a state $s$ and a decomposition $(A, B)\in \D$, \citeme constructs subsystems of $S$. 

\begin{definition}
    Given a system $(S, \D, T)$ in a state $s$, and a decomposition $(A,B)\in \D$, there is a \emph{subsystem} $(A, \D_{\mid A}, T_{\mid A})$, defined by the restricted decomposition set $\D_{\mid A}$ and a restricted time evolution $T_{\mid A}$ defined by the composition
    \[A\overset{1\otimes s_{\mid B}}\to A\otimes B \overset{T}\to A\otimes B \overset{1\otimes q_{A}}\to A.\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    These subsystems are again independent of the class representative of the decomposition $(A,B)$ in $\D$. 
\end{remark}




\subsection{Cause and effect structures}

Central to IIT is the cause and effect structures of the system $S$. This is what models how each part of the system can have a causal effect on other parts of the system. 

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{mechanism-purview pair} is a pair of subsystems of a given system $(S, \D, T)$, depending on the same global state $s\: \1 \to S$.    
\end{definition}

Intuitively a mechanism describes the subset of $S$ that we are ``letting make a difference'', and the purview describes the subset of $S$ that is affected. 

\begin{definition}
    Given a mechanism $(M, \D_{\mid M}, T_{\mid M})$ and a purview $(P,\D_{\mid P}, T_{\mid P})$, we define the \emph{cause} to be process 
    $$\cau\: M \overset{1\otimes w_{M'}}\to M\otimes M' \simeq S \overset{T}\to S \simeq P\otimes P' \overset{1\otimes q_{P'}}\to P,$$
    where $M'$ and $P'$ are the complements in the decompositions defined by $M$ and $P$ respectively. Similarly, the \emph{effect} is defined as
    $$\eff\: M \overset{1\otimes w_{M'}}\to M\otimes M' \simeq S \overset{T^\dagger}\to S \simeq P\otimes P' \overset{1\otimes q_{P'}}\to P.$$
\end{definition}

Note that both $\cau$ and $\eff$ send causal states to causal states. Intuitively, these capture the way the previous and next state of $P$ is constrained by the restricted state $s_{\mid M}$.  When the mechanism is the unit $\1$, then the cause and effect $\cau, \eff\: \1\to P$ are respectively called the \emph{unconstrained cause}, denoted $u\cau$ and the \emph{unconstrained effect}, denoted $u\eff$. 

\begin{definition}
    Given a state $s$ and a mechanism-purview pair $M,P$, the associated \emph{cause repertoir} is defined to be the state 
    $$\cau(s,M,P) = \1\overset{s_{\mid M}}\to M \overset{\cau\otimes u\cau}\to P\otimes P' \simeq S.$$
    One defines an \emph{effect repertoir} $\eff(s,M,P)$ similarly. 
\end{definition}

We also need to decompose and causes and effects.  

\begin{definition}
    Given a decomposition $(M_1, M_2)$ of a mechanism $M$, and a decomposition $(P_1, P_2)$ of a purview $P$, the \emph{decomposed} cause is the process 
    $$\cau_{M_1, M_2}^{P_1, P_2} \: M \simeq M_1\otimes M_2 \overset{\cau\otimes \cau}\to P_1\otimes P_2 \simeq P,$$
    and again similarly for defining the \emph{decomposed} effect.
\end{definition}

The \emph{decomposed} cause and effect repertoirs are then defined similarly to the normal ones, by replacing $\cau$ and $\eff$ with their decomposed variants in the definitions. We denote these states by $\cau_{M_1, M_2}^{P_1, P_2}(s,M,P)$ and $\eff_{M_1, M_2}^{P_1, P_2}(s,M,P)$ respectively. 

The following definitions will be key in defining our factorization presheaf later. 

\begin{definition}
    Given a state $s$ of a system $(S, \D, T)$, its \emph{cause-effect structure} is the set
    \[R_S = \{\cau(s,M,P), \eff(s,M,P)\}_{(M,P)}\]
    over all mechanism-purview pairs. Similarly, we define the cause-effect structure of a subsystem $(A,\D_{\mid A}, T_{\mid A})$ for a decomposition $(A,B)$. 
\end{definition}


\subsection{Directional cuts}

To relate our obstruction theoretic framework to classical IIT later, we will need to define cuts of systems. 

Given a system $(S, \D, T)$ and a decomposition $(A, B, \psi) \in \D$, one needs to be able to form systems that disconnects certain causal connections in the total system. IIT 3.0 \citeme does this by introducing \emph{cut systems}, that directionally disconnects causal flow from the two parts. IIT 4.0 \citeme does this in a slightly different manner, by replacing parts of the inputs of the time evolutions with independent noise. We have included versions of IIT 4.0's disconnections in arbitrary system states for reference and intuition, but we remark that the actual implementation of such disconnections does not matter much, as long as they are functorial. 

\begin{definition}
    Given a decomposition $(A, B, \psi) \in \D$, the \emph{left cut} replaces the contribution of $B$ to the time evolution $T$ with the noise state, meaning that only the contribution from $A$ matters. Formally, it is the system $(S, \D, T^{\psi, \leftarrow})$, where the new time evolution operator is defined as
    $$T^{\psi, \leftarrow}\: A\otimes B \overset{1\otimes w_B q_B}\to A\otimes B \overset{T}\to A\otimes B.$$
\end{definition}

In spirit this forces the time evolution to retain all self-influences from $A$, and influences from $A$ to $B$, but removes all influences from $B$ to $A$. The arrow in the notation is meant to denote the direction that is removed. 

We define the \emph{right cut} similarly by replacing $T$ with
\[T^{\psi,\rightarrow}\: A\otimes B \overset{w_A q_A\otimes 1}\to A\otimes B \overset{T}\to A\otimes B,\]
and the \emph{double cut} by the diagram 
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}
A\otimes B \arrow[d, "1\otimes w_A\otimes w_B\otimes 1"'] \arrow[r, "{T^{\psi, \leftrightarrow}}"] & A\otimes B                                                                  \\
A\otimes B\otimes A\otimes B \arrow[r, "T\otimes T"]                                               & A\otimes B\otimes A\otimes B \arrow[u, "1\otimes q_B\otimes q_A\otimes 1"']
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}

\begin{remark}
    The double cut corresponds to Tull--Kleiner's bidirectional cut, where one evolves the system as if $A$ and $B$ were completely disconnected from each other. 
\end{remark}

We will use the notation $T^{\psi, \alpha}$, with $\alpha \in \{\leftarrow, \rightarrow, \leftrightarrow\}$ to denote a general disconnection based on a decomposition $\psi\: S\simeq A\otimes B$. As we did above with the restricted time evolution, we also get definitions of restricted cuts due to the functoriality of discarding and noise; these are denoted $T^{\psi, \alpha}_{\mid A}$.  

Given a cut direction $\alpha$, we can also define cut cause and effect repertoirs, $\cau^\alpha(s,M,P)$ and $\eff^\alpha(s,M,P)$, by using the cut time evolution $T^{\psi, \alpha}$. Here $\psi$ is the decomposition associated to the mechanism $M$. Combining this with the decomposition of cause and effect repertoirs, gives \emph{cut-decomposed} cause and effect repertoirs. 





\section{Integrated cohomology}
\label{sec:integrated-cohomology}

In this section we present the more novel parts of this paper, which is a cohomology theory for IIT systems. We then build an obstruction theory for decomposing a physical system based on this cohomology theory, and show that it detects the existence of integrated information. Lastly we extend this to naturally define higher order integration, often referred to as higher \emph{synergy}. 

\subsection{Prerequisites}
In essence, cohomology groups are mathematical constructions that measure the amount of holes of different dimensions a space or other mathematical object has. It is generally defined using homological algebra, see \citeme for an introduction. 

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{cochain complex} $C^\bullet$ is a sequence of abelian groups 
    \[\cdots \to C^{i-1} \overset{\delta^{i-1}}\to C^i \overset{\delta^i}\to C^{i+1} \to \cdots\]
    such that $\delta^{i-1}\circ \delta^i = 0$ for all $i$.
\end{definition}

The the elements of $C^\bullet$ are called the \emph{$i$-cochains}. An $i$-cochain in the image of the map $\delta^{i-1}$ is called a \emph{coboundary}, while an $i$-cochain in the kernel of $\delta^i$ is called a \emph{cocycle}. The equation $\delta^{i-1}\circ \delta^i = 0$ implies that $\Ima(\delta^{i-1})\subseteq \Ker(\delta^i)$, which is the fundamental fact used to define cohomology -- in words, any coboundary is also a cocycle. 

\begin{definition}
    Given a cochain complex $C^\bullet$, its $i$'th \emph{cohomology group} is defined as the quotient group
    \[H^i(C^\bullet) := \Ker(\delta^{i})/\Ima(\delta^{i-1}).\]
    Elements of $H^i(C^\bullet)$ are called \emph{cohomology classes}. 
\end{definition}

One of the central goals of algebraic topology and algebraic geometry is to functorially assign cochain complexes to topological and geometric objects and compute the resulting homology groups. For a category of interest $\C$, for example topological spaces, manifolds or schemes, such assignments defines functors into the category of graded abelian groups $H^*\: \C \to \Ab_*$ called \emph{cohomology theories}. Examples include \emph{simplicial cohomology} \citeme, \emph{de Rham cohomology} \citeme, \emph{coherent cohomology} \citeme and \v{C}ech cohomology. It is the latter one we use in this paper, so let us quickly recall its construction. The reader is refered to \citeme for details.  

\begin{definition}
    Cech cohomology
\end{definition}




\subsection{The factorization presheaf}



\begin{definition}
    Let $(S,\D,T)$ be a system in a state $s$. We define the repertoir presheaf $\EuScript{R}$ to be the functor 
    \begin{align*}
        \EuScript{R}\: \Sub_s(S)\op &\to \Ab \\
        U &\longmapsto \Z[R_U]  
    \end{align*}
    mapping a subsystem $U$ to the free group on equivalence classes of cause-effect structures on $U$. Given a subsystem inclusion $V\hookrightarrow U$, the restriction map $\rho_U^V\: \EuScript{R}(U)\to \EuScript{R}(V)$ is defined as the equivalence class $[R_{U\mid V}]$, extended linearly.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    Let $r$ be a repertoir (either a cause or effect) and $(A,B)$ a decomposition of $S$. The restrictions
    \[r_{\mid A}\: \1 \to A \text{ and } r_{\mid B}\: \1\to B\]
    define a new state 
    \[r_{\mid A}\otimes r_{\mid B}\: \1\simeq 1\otimes 1 \to A\otimes B \simeq S\]
    which we call the \emph{factorized repertoir}. We say $r$ \emph{factors} over the decomposition $(A,B)$ if $r= r_{\mid A}\otimes r_{\mid B}$, and we say that $r$ is \emph{factorizable} if it factors over some decomposition of $S$. 
\end{definition}
